Security and privacy

On a PC with a TPM chip, you type only one password at startup, your account's, and yet the disk is encrypted. Here is what that protects, and what it doesn't.

Secure Boot

Raye starts with Secure Boot turned on and the keys most PCs ship with: on the test machine, I didn't have to change anything in the firmware. Some PCs, often sold as “Secured-core”, only accept Windows out of the box; you then have to allow the so-called “third-party” keys in the firmware settings. If your PC still refuses to start Raye, send me its model.

The encrypted disk

If you tick “Encrypt the Raye system” on the installer's Disk page, you pick a disk password, and the whole system is encrypted. Taken out of the PC and plugged in elsewhere, the disk can't be read without that password or the recovery key.

On a PC with a TPM chip, the chip opens the disk at startup. On that PC, your account password is therefore what protects your files, as with BitLocker. The chip only watches the Secure Boot settings: if they change, or the disk is put in another PC, Raye asks for the disk password again. Other changes in the startup don't ask for it. Without a TPM chip, the disk password is asked at every startup.

I tried it once on the test machine: a firmware update that revokes old startup keys, and the disk opened by itself afterwards. If an update of that kind installs along with others at a restart, you may be asked for the recovery key once.

The recovery key

At your first sign-in, a window shows a recovery key: eight groups of six digits. It opens the disk if you forget its password. Copy it, write it down on paper or in a password manager, keep it somewhere other than the computer, then confirm with “I saved my key”.

The password before an app changes the system

When an app wants to change the system, the screen dims and Raye asks for your password, with the publisher's name, as in Windows 11. The prompt doesn't time out: on a standard account, you have time to go and ask the PC's administrator for the password. Updates, on the other hand, install without asking for it.

What is still missing

  • The prompt sometimes names the technical program rather than the app asking.
  • You can't encrypt or decrypt the disk once Raye is installed yet: it's a choice made when installing.

If the PC crashes

The Raye kernel, which you turn on in Settings, Drivers, shows a violet screen with the Raye eye if the system crashes, then restarts by itself after 30 seconds. The original kernel stays installed and stays the default.

With Secure Boot, its key is enrolled at the next restart, in three steps: a blue screen shows up, press a key within 10 seconds, choose “Enroll MOK”, then type the six-digit code Settings showed you. If the 10 seconds go by, Raye starts on the original kernel.

What Raye sends over the Internet

Raye Update asks the two copies of Raye's packages, the base system's servers, Mozilla's for Firefox, and the firmware service for BIOS and device updates. The Store asks Flathub. The system also checks that the Internet answers, to spot a hotel network that wants you to sign in, and sets its clock from a time server. Firefox opens rayelinux.com when it starts, with no ads and no “managed by your organization” banner.

Two things only go out if you agreed to them: your location, when an app asks for it and you say yes, and anonymous usage statistics, a box left unticked on the installer's Privacy page.

This list comes from what I know of the code. I haven't yet watched a fresh install for an hour to check it address by address; once that's done, the record will be on this page.
